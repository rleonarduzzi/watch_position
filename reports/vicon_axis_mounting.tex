\section{Definitions}
Denote with ``optical body'' the rigid body tracked by Vicon.
Its position in the world frame of reference is $\mathbf{p}$ and its attitude is the quaternion
$q$. The quaternion encodes a rotation $R(q)$ from body to world reference frames
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{world}} = R(q)\,\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{body}} .
  \label{eq:body}
\end{equation}
The $z$ coordinate in the optical body frame is oriented upwards.

The IMU is expressed in a second frame of reference on the same object.

\section{Problem}
The IMU frame of reference was assumed to coincide with the ``optical'' frame of reference.
However, it was observed that while the IMU and optical $z$ axes match on all sequences, the $x$ and
$y$ axes seem to point in opposite directions. On these sequences, this inversion can be fixed by a
rotation of $\pi$ radians around the $z$ axis:
\begin{equation}
  R_z(\pi) = \operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,+1),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{body}} = R_z(\pi)\,\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{imu}} .
  \label{eq:rz}
\end{equation}

\section{Diagnostics Tests}
In this section we describe the tests used to uncover the problem.
We apply the tests to the first two minutes of $7$ recordings.


\subsection{Angular velocity test}
Denote $\boldsymbol{\omega}_k$ the $k$-th angular velocity sample, and with
$\Delta t = 0.01\,\mathrm{s}$ the sampling period.

%The optical body rate is the right-trivialised increment of $q$.
Over one sample the attitude advances by a body rotation,
\[
  q_{k+1} = q_k\otimes\exp(\boldsymbol{\omega}_k\Delta t),
\]
where $\exp$ denotes the exponential map that converts  the rotation vector
$\boldsymbol{\omega}_k\Delta t$ to a corresponding unit quaternion.
Left-multiplying by $q_k^{-1}$ we isolate the increment,
\[
  q_k^{-1}\otimes q_{k+1} = \exp(\boldsymbol{\omega}_k\Delta t).
\]
Inverting with the logarithmic map for a rotation of angle less than $\pi$, we obtain the
\textbf{angular velocity in the optical body frame} obtained from vicon:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\omega}_k
  = \frac{1}{\Delta t}\,\mathrm{Log}\bigl(q_k^{-1}\otimes q_{k+1}\bigr) .
  \label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}

We want to compare this with  IMU angular velocities in the IMU body frame,
$\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mathrm{imu}}_k$.

First, we smooth the IMU measurements with a local average,
$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_k =
\tfrac12\bigl(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mathrm{imu}}_k +
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mathrm{imu}}_{k+1}\bigr)$.
Further, we consider only those samples with a large enough velocity,
$\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\| > 0.5\,\mathrm{rad/s}$.
Then, we compute the per-axis correlations
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wcorr}
\boldsymbol{c} = \mathrm{corr}_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{k}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_k)
\end{equation}

Large and negative correlations in the $x$ and $y$ axes indicate that these axes are
inverted. Moreover, this can be used as a criterion to decide when to apply the correction
(\ref{eq:rz}) to reverse the axes:
\begin{equation}
  \text{reverse } x,y
  \iff
  -c_x - c_y + c_z > c_x + c_y + c_z
  \iff
  c_x + c_y < 0 .
  \label{eq:test}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fix:gyros_ok} shows a sequence where the IMU and optical frames agree: the two angular
velocities $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ overlap.
On the contrary, figure \ref{fix:gyros_wrong} shows a case where both frames differ: the $x$ and $y$
coordinates of both angular velocities clearly differ, while the $z$ coordinate remains the same.

\subsection{Acceleration test}
First, we compute the ``optical specific force'' $\mathbf{f}$ as the second-derivative of the
optical position $\mathbf{p}$
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{f}_k
  = \frac{\mathbf{p}_{k+1} - 2\mathbf{p}_k + \mathbf{p}_{k-1}}{\Delta t^2}
  + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ g \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:force}
\end{equation}
This specific force is expressed in the world frame of reference.
%We smooth this sequence with a 9-sample moving average.

Then, we express the IMU acceleration $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{imu}}$ in the world frame of reference.
This implies rotating with the correction matrix $R_z$ from (\ref{eq:rz}), if needed, and then
with the rotation matrix $R(q)$. We write the world-frame acceleration as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{world}} = R(q)\,R\,\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{imu}}.
  \qquad R \in \{I,\, R_z(\pi)\} .
  \label{eq:acc}
\end{equation}

Figures~\ref{fig:acc_ok} and \ref{fig:acc_wrong} show the behavior of the specific force when using $R=I$.
For an aligned sequence, Figure~\ref{fig:acc_ok} shows that both traces overlap, while
Figure~\ref{fig:acc_wrong} shows that the traces differ on an unaligned sequence:
the vertical component of the accelerometer sits near $-g$ while $f_z$ sits near $+g$, while the
other two components are not mirrored due to the effect of $R(q)$.

\begin{figure}[H]

Once more, a numerical test to determine the need to correct can be based on correlations.
First compute the correlation coefficients between both measurements, and then average them over all
components:
\begin{align}
\nonumber
  \mathbf{\gamma} &=\mathrm{corr}(\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{world}}, \mathbf{f})\\
  \Gamma &= \frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^3\mathbf{\gamma}_i.
\label{eq:acorr}
\end{align}
The correlation $\Gamma$ can be computed in the two cases, $R=I$ and $R=R_z$. If the correlation for
the second case is larger than for the first one, then the correction is needed.

\section{Roll and pitch from the accelerometer}
At rest the accelerometer reads the specific force of gravity in the body frame,
\[
  \mathbf{a} = R(q)^{\mathsf T}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ g \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Write the optical attitude as the intrinsic Z-Y-X rotation
\(R(q) = R_z(\psi)\,R_y(\theta)\,R_x(\phi)\),
with roll \(\phi\), pitch \(\theta\) and yaw \(\psi\).
A yaw about the lab vertical leaves \((0,0,g)^{\mathsf T}\) unchanged, so \(\psi\) drops out and
\begin{align*}
  a_x &= -g\sin\theta, \\
  a_y &= g\cos\theta\sin\phi, \\
  a_z &= g\cos\theta\cos\phi.
\end{align*}
For \(\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) one has \(\cos\theta\ge 0\), and the two angles are
\begin{equation}
  \phi = \operatorname{atan2}(a_y, a_z),
  \qquad
  \theta = \operatorname{atan2}\bigl(-a_x,\, \sqrt{a_y^2 + a_z^2}\bigr).
  \label{eq:tilt}
\end{equation}
Replacing \((a_x, a_y)\) by \((-a_x, -a_y)\), which is what \(R_z(\pi)\) does, changes \((\phi,
\theta)\) to \((-\phi, -\theta)\).

Note that the formulas hold when \(\mathbf{a}\) is gravity.
We can consider them approximate under mild accelerations, but they will become invalid  during hard
motion.
Since roll is read from the two components $a_y$ and  $a_z$, it is estimated from a projection of
gravity with weight $\cos\theta$.
When the optical pitch is large, that projection shrinks while the linear acceleration does not, so
the accelerometer roll departs from the optical roll, making it unusable.
On the contrary, pitch is read from $a_x = -g\sin\theta$ which holds most of $g$ when the pitch
grows,  so it continues to follow the optical pitch.
For this reason, in analysis below we focus only on pitch.

We evaluated these pitch estimate from the accelerometer, and compared it with pitch of the optical
quaternion.
We focus on the first $10\,\mathrm{s}$ of data, where $\|\mathbf{a}\|$ is closest to $\mathbf{g}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:euler_acc} shows that both estimates match when the sequence doesn't require
inversion, while otherwise they become inverted as well.

\section{Correction}
When \eqref{eq:test} holds, the measurements are mapped into the optical body and the pose is left as recorded:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{a} \leftarrow R_z(\pi)\,\mathbf{a},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol{\omega} \leftarrow R_z(\pi)\,\boldsymbol{\omega},
  \qquad
  q \text{ and } \mathbf{p} \text{ unchanged}.
  \label{eq:fix}
\end{equation}
\texttt{load\_trial} applies \eqref{eq:fix} in memory.
\texttt{scripts/fix\_vicon\_axes.py} writes the same sign flips into the CSV.
A file that already satisfies $c_x + c_y \ge 0$ is not rewritten.

\section{Effect of the correction}
The two tests fix the mounting from the gyroscope and the accelerometer alone.
To see what the horizontal inversion does to an attitude error, the same prefixes are given to an estimator, once with the axes as recorded and once after \eqref{eq:fix}.
The estimator is VQF, in 6-axis mode with $\tau_{\mathrm{acc}}=3$, the value fixed on BROAD and not retuned here.
It was selected because it does not depend on the filter derived in this report, so a change in its error is evidence about the axes.

The score is the inclination.
VQF has no magnetometer, so its heading differs from the optical heading by an arbitrary constant and a slow drift, and the total rotation error folds that unobservable part into the number.
Inclination does not.
The lab error quaternion is $\hat{q}\otimes q^{-1} = (w,x,y,z)$, and
\begin{equation}
  \delta = 2\arccos\sqrt{w^{2}+z^{2}}
  \label{eq:incl}
\end{equation}
is the angle between the upward lab axis and the direction $R(\hat{q}\otimes q^{-1})$ assigns to it.
A heading is a rotation about that axis, and for a pure heading $\delta = 0$.
A lab yaw $q_z(\psi)\otimes\hat{q}$ mixes $w$ with $z$ and $x$ with $y$, so it leaves $\delta$ unchanged, and removing the best constant heading offset does not alter the score.
The inversion is a body yaw $\hat{q}\otimes q_z(\pi)$.
Once the object tilts, that yaw is no longer a rotation about the lab vertical, and $\delta$ moves, which is why the heading alignment does not hide the wrong mounting.
